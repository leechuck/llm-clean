# Identity (I)

A property **carries an identity condition** when *being that kind of thing* tells you what counts as one and the same thing. It answers two questions: given two instances, are they the same one or two different ones? And given one instance at two different times, is it still the same one?

Three qualifications:

1. **Identity, not identification.** DNA, VINs, and account numbers are epistemic aids for recognizing *which* one something is. The question is whether the kind itself determines sameness — identical twins share DNA and are two persons.
2. **Inherited counts.** A class is `+I` if it supplies its own criterion *or* inherits one from any superclass. *Student* adds nothing about identity but is subsumed by *Person*, so it is `+I`. Identity is inherited downward and never lost.
3. **Non-circular.** "x is the same as y" is not a criterion. The rule must appeal to something other than identity itself.

**`+I`** — instances can be counted and tracked as instances of that kind: *Person*, *Organism*, *Statue*, *Building*, *Event*, *Number*, *Contract*, and *Amount of Matter* (same matter, same amount — it carries identity even though it is not a unified whole; mass terms typically fail **unity**, not identity).

**`−I`** — the property is truly predicated of things but supplies no way to individuate them: *Red*, *Large*, *Valuable*, *Damaged*, *Entity*/*Thing* (applies to everything, distinguishes nothing). These presuppose some other class to do the counting.

**Test:** ask "how many Xs?" with no further context. If answerable → `+I`. If you must first supply another noun — "how many red *cars*" — → `−I`. Where articulating the criterion is hard, the counting test still resolves the case; a contested criterion (*Person*) is still a criterion.
